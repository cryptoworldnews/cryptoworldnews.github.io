Ethereum Beacon Chain: How Its Validator Proofs Enable Restaking

What is the Beacon Chain in Ethereum? It runs proof of stake, tracks validators and stakes, and supplies the balance proofs native restaking needs.

Merkle Street NewsroomNov 25, 2026 · 08:09 UTC#7bf8553 min read

The Ethereum Beacon Chain is the proof-of-stake consensus layer that tracks validators and helps Ethereum agree on blocks. It records validator balances and withdrawal credentials, which native restaking uses to show that ETH is staked. That connection also matters when weighing eigen layer’s added restaking risks: EigenLayer can put a validator’s stake to work securing additional services, with potential rewards and slashing risk.

Think of Ethereum as having two linked jobs. The execution layer processes transactions and runs smart contracts. The Beacon Chain coordinates proof of stake: it keeps track of validators, assigns their duties, and organizes agreement on blocks. Since The Merge, Ethereum uses both layers together.

What does the Beacon Chain do?

The Beacon Chain coordinates validators and block agreement through slots and epochs. A slot lasts 12 seconds, and each slot gives an assigned validator the opportunity to propose a block. Other validators can attest to the block they consider valid. An epoch is 32 slots, or about 6.4 minutes, and groups those duties into a larger time interval.

Validators stake ETH to take part. The familiar 32 ETH is the stake required to activate a validator; the validator’s balance can change over time. The Beacon Chain tracks that balance and the validator’s withdrawal credentials. Those credentials specify where withdrawn stake can go.

Finality is the network’s agreement that a block and the blocks before it are settled under proof of stake. Validators’ attestations help Ethereum reach that agreement. The Beacon Chain launched on 1 December 2020, initially running alongside the proof-of-work Ethereum chain. At The Merge, on 15 September 2022, it joined Ethereum’s execution layer and became the consensus layer for the network.

This is why the Beacon Chain is not a separate network that users need to switch to today. It is the consensus part of Ethereum, working alongside the execution layer. A transaction is handled on the execution side; the Beacon Chain coordinates the validators who agree on the blocks containing it.

Why does native restaking use it?

Native restaking uses a validator’s Beacon Chain stake and withdrawal credentials. Say you operate an Ethereum validator and opt to restake it through EigenLayer. The validator continues doing its regular job on the Beacon Chain. You point its withdrawal credentials to an EigenPod, the contract used to connect that validator to EigenLayer.

The EigenPod needs evidence that the validator exists and has a particular balance. EIP-4788 gives execution-layer contracts access to a beacon block root: a cryptographic commitment to Beacon Chain data. The root is not the balance itself. It gives a contract a reference against which a proof about the validator’s balance can be checked.

In practice, a restaker supplies a proof showing the validator’s balance in Beacon Chain state. The EigenPod checks that proof against the beacon block root made available through EIP-4788. When it verifies, EigenLayer can count the proven ETH as restaked. The root connects the two layers: the balance is tracked by the Beacon Chain, while the EigenPod and restaking contracts operate on Ethereum’s execution layer.

That proof path means EigenPod balance checks can use Ethereum’s own beacon block root rather than relying on an external oracle to report the balance. The practical point is that native restaking follows the validator’s on-chain stake and withdrawal credentials. The validator stays on the Beacon Chain; its credentials point to an EigenPod; and a proof tied to the beacon block root lets that pod verify the balance.
