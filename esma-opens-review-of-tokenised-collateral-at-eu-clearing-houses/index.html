ESMA opens review of tokenised collateral at EU clearing houses

ESMA opened a call for evidence on tokenised collateral at EU clearing houses, asking whether existing safeguards can handle its transfer, custody and use in defaults.

Merkle Street NewsroomOct 10, 2026 · 10:33 UTC#e65d9d3 min read

ESMA opened a call for evidence on 9 October on whether EU central counterparties can safely use tokenised collateral, putting its transfer and recovery in a member default under scrutiny. A central counterparty, or CCP, collects margin and default fund contributions from clearing members to cover losses if one fails. ESMA’s announcement of the call says responses are due by 15 January 2027.

The mechanism starts with a clearing member selecting eligible assets to meet its obligations. The member then moves them through custody, settlement and collateral management systems to the CCP. The CCP values the assets, applies haircuts and monitors eligibility. If a member defaults, the CCP needs to access the collateral and turn it into usable funds in time to manage the resulting exposure. ESMA’s call for evidence asks how tokenisation could change each step.

What tokenised collateral models is ESMA examining?

ESMA describes two main models, plus arrangements that combine them. A “digital twin” is a token that represents or helps use an asset still held and recorded in traditional market infrastructure. The token may mirror rights recorded elsewhere; ESMA says firms would need to know which record controls if the records disagree.

In the second model, an asset is issued, held and transferred on a distributed ledger. Collateral processes such as posting and monitoring take place in that ledger environment. ESMA says arrangements may also interact with tokenised cash, including tokenised central bank money, tokenised deposits or stablecoins, and with other settlement mechanisms. A digital twin is like a tracking label attached to an asset held in a vault: the label can help identify it, but access to the underlying asset still depends on the vault’s systems.

What must happen if a clearing member defaults?

The CCP must be able to reach the collateral, transfer it and convert it into liquidity. For a digital twin, ESMA says that route may still depend on custodians or central securities depositories. Reconciliation and confirmation between the ledger and traditional systems could add steps before the asset can be sold, used in a repo or otherwise realised.

With a natively issued asset, the route to value may run through on-ledger transfers or sales. Its speed and reliability would depend on available liquidity, settlement mechanisms and any external intermediaries. In both models, the key test is whether the CCP can make the asset usable in stressed conditions, including after a member default.

Does the call change which assets CCPs can accept?

No. ESMA says the exercise is not intended to expand or reassess the categories of eligible collateral. It is asking whether tokenisation changes how already eligible assets are mobilised, transferred, managed, protected and realised, and whether current EU rules remain adequate.

The regulator is seeking evidence on legal certainty, client protection, segregation, settlement finality, liquidity, haircuts and operational resilience. ESMA says it will assess responses in the first quarter of 2027 and then decide whether regulatory or supervisory action within its remit is appropriate. The call is an evidence-gathering step; it does not announce approval for CCPs to use tokenised collateral.
