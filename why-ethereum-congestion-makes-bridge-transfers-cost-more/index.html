Why Ethereum Congestion Makes Bridge Transfers Cost More

Ethereum fees rise when blockspace is scarce, while a bridge can add separate costs on both chains. Learn what each charge pays for before moving tokens.

Merkle Street NewsroomOct 23, 2026 · 12:27 UTC#e7efb34 min read

Ethereum congestion raises the cost of getting a transaction into a block, and a bridge transfer can require separate transactions on two networks. First, your wallet builds and signs a transaction, such as a token approval or transfer. Ethereum measures the computation that transaction needs in units called gas. You pay for the gas used, multiplied by the gas price, in ETH.

Each block has a base fee, which changes with recent demand for blockspace, plus an optional priority fee, or tip, that can make a transaction more attractive to a validator. When demand pushes blocks above their target size, the protocol raises the base fee. A busy period can therefore make even a simple transfer cost more, while a contract call that does more work also uses more gas. Ethereum’s base fee is burned; the validator receives the tip.

Why does Ethereum congestion increase gas fees?

Congestion increases the price users must pay to compete for limited space in upcoming blocks. Think of a block as a delivery van with limited room: each transaction takes up some space, and users can offer a higher tip when they want their transaction picked sooner. The analogy ends there; on Ethereum, the base fee is set by the protocol and the validator chooses which eligible transactions to include.

Your wallet usually estimates a fee using current conditions, but that estimate can change before a transaction is included. A transaction with a fee cap below the base fee cannot be included at that price. One with a low tip may wait while higher-tip transactions are selected. If you are not in a hurry, waiting for demand to ease can reduce the fee, but it cannot guarantee a lower price or a particular confirmation time.

What extra costs can a bridge add?

A bridge transfer can involve a fee on the source network, bridge-specific charges, and a transaction on the destination network. The source transaction may approve a token contract and then deposit or lock tokens; some flows combine steps, while others need separate signatures. The bridge’s contracts or operators then verify the transfer according to the bridge design, and the destination side releases, mints, or routes the corresponding asset.

That second network has its own blockspace and fee market. A transfer from Ethereum to Polygon, for example, may incur an Ethereum fee to start the transfer and a later fee on Polygon to use or move the asset there. If you need the walkthrough of the steps and token handling, this guide to using Polygon Bridge for token transfers covers that process in more detail.

Check the quote before signing. It should show the amount arriving, the network used for each step, and any bridge charge or waiting period. The displayed total can differ from the source-chain gas fee alone. A bridge also adds contract or operator risk: a transfer depends on the bridge design as well as the networks it connects.

How can you estimate the full cost before sending?

Estimate the whole route, not just the first fee shown in your wallet. Before you approve or send, check these parts:

  • Source gas: the cost of approvals, deposits, or other transactions on the starting network.
  • Bridge charge: any stated fee, exchange spread, or liquidity cost for the route.
  • Destination gas: funds needed to claim, receive, or use the asset on the destination network.
  • Asset and network: the token representation and destination chain the bridge will deliver.

For most beginners, the better choice is to compare the amount that will arrive and the number of steps, not to chase the lowest advertised fee. A cheaper route may take longer or rely on different bridge security assumptions. If the Ethereum portion is expensive and the transfer is not urgent, waiting for lower demand can help; if the destination transaction needs its own gas token, account for that before moving funds. The key is simple: Ethereum gas pays for computation on Ethereum, and bridging can add separate costs and risks at each end of the route.
