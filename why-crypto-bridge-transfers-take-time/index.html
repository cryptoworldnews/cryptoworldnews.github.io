Why Crypto Bridge Transfers Take Time

Bridge wait times depend on source-chain finality, bridge design and destination processing; tracking each step helps distinguish normal delay from a stuck transfer.

Merkle Street NewsroomOct 16, 2026 · 18:29 UTC#7389834 min read

Crypto bridge wait times depend on how quickly the source chain confirms a transfer, how the bridge verifies it, and when the destination chain processes it. A transfer can look idle even after one part is complete because the bridge moves assets through several separate steps. Knowing which step is underway gives you a better estimate than a single countdown.

What happens when crypto moves across a bridge?

A bridge first receives your request to move an asset from one network to another. In a lock-and-mint design, a contract holds the original tokens on the source chain; after the bridge verifies the deposit, a contract on the destination chain issues a corresponding token. In a burn-and-release design, the source token is destroyed or taken out of circulation, then an equivalent amount is released on the destination. Other bridges route through liquidity held on both networks, so they may exchange assets rather than move the same token representation.

The source chain must record the transaction before the bridge can act on it. The bridge then checks that record. Depending on its design, that check may involve validators, a relayer, or another verification system. Once the destination chain accepts the resulting instruction, it records the release, mint, or swap. It is a little like sending a parcel through a depot: the sender’s receipt, the depot’s check, and the final handoff happen at different points.

Some guides cover the bridge and swap sequence in more detail; for a worked example, see Biswap’s guide to bridging and swapping crypto. A swap may add another transaction after the bridge, so its completion time can differ from the bridge transfer itself.

Why can one transfer take longer than another?

The wait depends on both networks and the bridge. A congested source chain can take longer to include a transaction. The bridge may wait for additional confirmations before treating that transaction as settled, which reduces the chance that a later chain reorganization reverses it. Different bridges set different verification rules, and destination processing can add another wait.

Liquidity bridges have a different constraint: the destination needs enough of the relevant asset available to complete the transfer. A route may wait, use another path, or fail if available liquidity or route conditions change. A bridge that shows a source transaction as complete may still be waiting for its destination step.

Fees can affect timing too. On networks where transaction fees influence inclusion order, a low fee may leave a transaction waiting. A bridge can also batch or queue work, so its own processing does not always begin the instant the source transaction appears on-chain. The displayed estimate is a guide based on expected conditions, not a guarantee.

How can you tell what stage a bridge transfer has reached?

Start with the transaction hash shown by the bridge and open it in the source network’s block explorer. Check whether it is pending, confirmed, or failed. Then return to the bridge’s status page and look for a destination transaction hash or a message that verification is still in progress. If a destination hash appears, check that transaction on the destination network’s explorer.

  • Source transaction pending: The source network has not included it yet. The bridge cannot finish its checks.
  • Source confirmed, bridge processing: The bridge is verifying the deposit or waiting for its required confirmations.
  • Destination transaction pending: The bridge has submitted the next step, but the destination network has not completed it.
  • Destination transaction confirmed: Check the destination wallet and make sure it is set to the correct network and token.

If the status stops changing beyond the estimate, compare the bridge’s status page with both transaction records before taking action. Do not submit the transfer again just because the destination wallet has not updated; a second request could move more funds. If the source transaction failed, or the bridge reports an error, use the bridge’s stated recovery process and confirm the destination network and token contract before adding a token manually.

The practical rule is to track the source transaction, the bridge’s verification, and the destination transaction as separate events. That sequence explains most waits and shows where to look when a transfer needs help.
