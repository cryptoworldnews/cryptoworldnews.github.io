Why Treasury Must Compare Net Value Before Bridging

Treasury should compare what arrives after bridge fees, swaps, slippage and destination costs, because the amount sent is not the amount available to use.

Merkle Street NewsroomOct 16, 2026 · 03:17 UTC#8a5a404 min read

Treasury should compare the value it can use after a bridge transfer with the value it sends, because fees, swaps and destination costs change the result. The process starts with a source wallet signing a transaction. Depending on the bridge, a contract may lock or burn the asset, then validators or other message systems confirm the transfer. A destination contract releases or creates an asset there. If the treasury needs a different token, a swap adds another step before the funds are ready.

Each step can change the amount or the asset’s market value. The bridge may charge a fee, the swap may execute at a worse price than quoted, and the destination wallet may need native tokens to pay for its next transaction. For a fuller sequence of pre-move checks, see this guide to super bridge transfer checks before funds move; it covers questions a treasury team should settle before signing.

What does net-delivered value include?

Net-delivered value is the usable amount received at the destination, after costs and execution effects. Start with the amount the source wallet will send. Subtract the bridge fee and any source-side transaction cost. Then account for the destination amount after any required swap, including its fee and price impact. Finally, include destination gas if the treasury must acquire it before it can use or move the asset.

Keep units clear. A quoted fee in one token cannot be subtracted directly from an amount in another; convert costs to a shared unit using the same price basis. Separate costs paid in tokens from costs paid in cash, and record whether the destination asset is the original token, a wrapped representation, or a different token obtained through a swap. That distinction matters for accounting and for what the treasury can do next.

How should treasury compare bridge routes?

Compare routes by the same starting amount, destination asset and valuation time. A route with a lower stated bridge fee may deliver less if it uses a costly swap or has greater price impact. A route with a higher fee can leave more value available if its execution is better. The comparison should use a quote for the full path, not just the bridge’s fee display.

For each route, record:

  • Amount and asset leaving the source wallet.
  • Bridge fee and source transaction cost.
  • Expected destination amount after any swap, including swap fees and price impact.
  • Destination gas or other costs required before the funds are usable.

Then compare the expected net amount in a common unit. Check the quote’s expiry and any assumptions about execution. If a route depends on a swap, the final amount may differ from the quoted estimate when the trade executes. An order book route depends on available orders; an automated market maker route depends on the pool and trade size. Treasury should use a conservative estimate when the quote can move before execution.

What can change the better choice?

The best route is the one that delivers the required asset and leaves the most usable value under the treasury’s constraints. Those constraints may include timing, liquidity, counterparty exposure, supported tokens and reconciliation requirements. A bridge route that delivers a token the treasury cannot use directly may require another swap or transfer, adding cost and operational work.

Before signing, verify the source and destination networks, token contract addresses, destination wallet and expected asset. Confirm the treasury can account for the asset received and has a plan for destination gas. These checks catch mismatches that a favorable quote alone cannot resolve.

Think of the transfer like shipping funds through a series of toll gates: the amount dispatched is only the starting figure. Treasury gets a useful comparison by tracing every fee and conversion to the amount that arrives ready for its intended purpose.
