Destination Execution Failure After Bridging Explained

A destination execution failure means the bridge transfer and the action after it had different outcomes. Your source-chain transaction may have finalized, while a swap or other instruction on the destination chain reverted, timed out, or never ran.

Keep three points in mind:

  • A failed destination action does not by itself prove your bridged funds are lost.
  • Where the assets ended up depends on the bridge’s delivery and recovery design.
  • Check both chains before retrying, and budget for any destination gas needed to recover or swap.

What failed, and where are the funds?

In a cross-chain flow, the source transaction locks, burns, or sends assets, and a later destination transaction delivers them or calls another contract. Those are separate transactions, so source confirmation does not guarantee that a destination swap succeeds.

For example, Maya bridges ETH to an L2 and asks for it to be swapped into a token. The bridge delivery could complete, but the swap could revert because its minimum output was no longer available, the route failed, or the destination call lacked gas. Depending on the design, Maya may receive the bridged asset without the swap, find funds claimable through a recovery path, or need to wait for another execution attempt.

There is no universal refund rule. Some systems make delivery and the requested action part of one destination call; if that call reverts, the contract may retain assets for a retry or claim. Other designs deliver the asset first and treat the swap as a separate action. Read the transaction outcome and the bridge’s documented recovery rules before assuming either an automatic refund or permanent loss.

How can you tell whether delivery or the swap failed?

Trace the operation in order: source transaction, bridge message or attestation, destination transaction, then any swap call. A source transaction marked successful only confirms that its own chain accepted it; look for a destination transaction receipt and its status to see whether execution actually happened.

Use the relevant network’s block explorer, such as Etherscan for Ethereum or Arbiscan for Arbitrum, to inspect transaction status, timestamps, and contract events. A destination transaction marked failed usually means its state changes were reverted, though gas may still have been spent. If no destination transaction exists, the operation may still be waiting on confirmations, message delivery, or a relayer; a delay is not the same as a revert.

Biswap is relevant when the task is to bridge and swap across networks in one flow: the Biswap bridge provides that cross-chain movement and exchange. The same distinction still matters when comparing any route: identify whether the swap is part of destination execution or a separate step, and what the service says happens if that step fails.

What should you do before trying again?

First, record the source and destination transaction hashes, the networks, token amounts, and timestamps. Then check whether the destination asset arrived in your wallet, remains in a contract, or is marked claimable; use the bridge’s stated recovery process for that specific state.

Do not submit the same bridge transfer again just because the swap failed. If the asset has already arrived, a new transfer could move it twice; if recovery requires a claim or retry, that action may need destination-chain gas. Also account for gas already consumed by a reverted transaction, which is generally not restored.

Before choosing a route, compare how it handles the failure state, how you can verify it, and whether you can cover another destination transaction. The question to ask yourself before acting is: if delivery succeeds but the swap reverts, do I know where the asset will be and how I can recover or exchange it?
