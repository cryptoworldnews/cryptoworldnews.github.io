US gets court authority over $1.2bn of Binance theft

The Justice Department says courts authorized seizure or restraint of $1.2 billion from Binance’s $6.3 billion theft, while tracing and freezes continue.

Merkle Street NewsroomNov 15, 2026 · 11:14 UTC#5937733 min read

The U.S. government has obtained court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, the Justice Department said on Nov. 12. That is one part of a wider effort: the department says U.S. agencies and private-sector partners have frozen more than $2.7 billion of the stolen assets so far.

The sequence began with the theft. According to the Justice Department’s announcement, members of the North Korean military stole more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance on or about Oct. 29. The FBI refers to the actors as “TraderTraitor.” The department says law enforcement teams then worked with virtual asset service providers, or VASPs, to trace and interdict the funds.

A freeze and a seizure are different steps. A freeze blocks assets from being moved, while a court-authorized seizure or restraint gives the government legal authority to take control of specified assets or prevent their transfer. The $1.2 billion figure is the amount covered by court authorization. It is not the total frozen, and the announcement does not say that every dollar in that amount has been forfeited or returned to Binance.

The moving assets make the work harder. The Justice Department says TraderTraitor actors continue to launder the stolen cryptocurrency through bridges, mixers and exchanges. Bridges move tokens between blockchain networks; mixers obscure transaction trails; exchanges can convert or hold digital assets. The FBI has urged VASPs to block transactions from addresses the actors are using, including transactions derived from those addresses. That gives service providers a role alongside investigators: they can help stop funds from moving through platforms they operate.

The next steps are still underway. The department says efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the assets continue. Forfeiture would put ownership before a judge. The Associated Press reports that, in a typical case, the government files a forfeiture claim and a judge determines who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds. That process is separate from the freeze: it addresses who ultimately gets the assets, not just whether they can be moved now.

The figures describe different stages of the response. More than $2.7 billion has been frozen through government and private-sector efforts; more than $1.2 billion of that has court authorization for seizure or restraint. Investigators and service providers are still tracing the remaining stolen funds, and the final amount returned, if any, will depend on the continuing recovery and forfeiture process.
