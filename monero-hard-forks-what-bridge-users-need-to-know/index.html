Monero Hard Forks: What Bridge Users Need to Know

Monero hard forks change the rules its nodes accept; bridge users need to track software, deposit confirmations and withdrawal status around an upgrade.

Merkle Street NewsroomSep 30, 2026#a49fce3 min read

A Monero hard fork changes the rules that nodes use to accept blocks and transactions, so bridge users need to know which software the bridge supports before moving XMR. Monero’s scheduled upgrades generally ask participants to update their software; they do not automatically create a second coin or split balances. The bridge adds another set of moving parts: it watches the Monero chain, waits for a deposit to meet its confirmation policy, then arranges the corresponding action on another network. For a step-by-step explanation of ZeroFi’s XMR bridge flow, see the separate guide.

What changes in a Monero hard fork?

At the upgrade point, updated nodes begin applying new consensus rules, while software that has not been updated may reject the new blocks or fail to follow the current chain. A wallet builds and broadcasts transactions under the rules it knows; a node checks them; miners add valid transactions to blocks. The change can affect transaction construction, validation, or other protocol behavior. Monero’s official FAQ says upgrades are now less frequent than the former twice-yearly schedule, and its last listed hard fork was in August 2022.

Think of it as a road authority changing the rules at a marked junction: vehicles using the current map can continue, while an old map may send a driver the wrong way. The analogy stops there. On Monero, nodes enforce rules with software, and a transaction must satisfy those rules to be accepted.

How can a hard fork affect an XMR bridge transfer?

A bridge often has a Monero-side watcher and a component that issues or releases an asset on another chain. When a user sends XMR to a deposit address, the watcher looks for the transaction and waits for the required confirmations. After the deposit qualifies, the bridge’s other side processes the matching credit. In the reverse direction, a bridge may receive a burn or withdrawal request on the other chain and arrange an XMR payment.

During an upgrade, the watcher’s node, wallet software, and transaction services must all understand the active Monero rules. If one component is behind, deposits may take longer to recognize or withdrawals may pause while operators update and verify their systems. Confirmation policies are set by the bridge, so a transaction visible in a wallet does not by itself mean the bridge has completed its side of the transfer.

What should bridge users check before sending XMR?

Check the bridge’s current status and its instructions for any announced Monero upgrade. Then check the deposit address and network shown for your specific transfer. These are the practical checks that matter:

  • Confirm that the bridge accepts XMR deposits or withdrawals during the upgrade window.
  • Check its confirmation requirements and wait for the bridge to mark the deposit as credited.
  • Keep the transaction details and destination address so you can track the transfer with the bridge’s support process.
  • For self-custody, use wallet software that supports the current Monero network before creating a transaction.

Does a Monero hard fork put bridge funds at risk?

A routine, scheduled upgrade does not by itself erase XMR or create a new balance that must be claimed. The bridge introduces a separate operational dependency: its software and operators must keep pace with Monero and accurately track deposits and payouts. A contentious split would raise a different question about which chain a service recognizes, so users should look for an explicit policy if one is announced.

For most users, the better choice is to avoid starting a bridge transfer during a maintenance window unless the service says it is processing normally. Wait for the bridge to confirm receipt before treating a deposit as complete, and check withdrawal status until the XMR reaches your wallet.
