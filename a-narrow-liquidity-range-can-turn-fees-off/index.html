A Narrow Liquidity Range Can Turn Fees Off

A concentrated-liquidity position earns swap fees only while its price stays in range, so a tighter band can raise fee share and then switch it off.

Merkle Street NewsroomOct 22, 2026 · 09:53 UTC#733c314 min read

A narrow liquidity range can increase a position’s share of trading fees while the market price stays inside it, then stop those fees when the price leaves. In a concentrated-liquidity pool, a provider deposits two tokens into a smart contract and chooses a lower and upper price. The contract uses that deposit to facilitate swaps within the chosen range. When traders swap, the pool charges a fee and allocates it among the liquidity positions that are active at the current price.

“Active” means the pool price is between the position’s two limits. A narrower range concentrates the deposit over fewer prices, so it can represent more of the available liquidity there. But if the price crosses either limit, the position stops participating in swaps at that price. For a fuller account of how pool versions handle this design, see biswap.

How does a narrow liquidity range earn fees?

A narrow range earns fees by placing more of a provider’s capital near the current price. First, the provider selects the token pair and deposits the required assets. Next, the provider sets the range. The pool’s contract then makes that liquidity available to traders while the market price sits inside the limits. Each eligible swap generates a fee, and the provider’s share depends on their active liquidity relative to the other liquidity available at that price.

Think of the range as a stall open only on certain streets: more activity on those streets can mean more customers, but traffic elsewhere brings none. The analogy ends there. In a pool, the contract tracks the price and whether each position is active; the provider does not manually approve each trade.

What happens when the price leaves the range?

When the price moves below the lower limit or above the upper limit, the position becomes inactive and stops earning swap fees until the price returns to its range. Its token balance also shifts as swaps trade against the position. At one boundary, it can end up holding only one of the pair’s tokens. That balance remains exposed to price changes, even while it earns no fees.

A narrow range therefore trades coverage for concentration. It can put capital to work efficiently around a price the provider expects to persist, but a larger price move can leave the position idle. A wider range covers more possible prices and may need less frequent adjustment, though the same deposit is spread across more prices and may account for a smaller share of liquidity near the current one.

How should a provider choose a range?

Choose a range based on how much time and attention you can give the position, as well as your view of likely price movement. A range that looks attractive on a screen can lose that advantage if the market quickly moves beyond it. Before depositing, consider:

  • How far the price could move before you can review the position.
  • Whether you are comfortable holding either token if the position becomes one-sided.
  • How often you are willing to adjust the limits and pay any related transaction costs.
  • Whether expected fee income justifies the risk of being inactive and the possibility of losses against simply holding the tokens.

Repositioning is not automatic in every pool. A provider may need to remove liquidity, set new limits, and deposit again; those actions can incur transaction costs and change the token mix. Fee income also does not by itself show whether the position beat holding the same assets, because price movement and costs affect the comparison.

Is a narrow range better for most providers?

For providers who cannot monitor prices or reposition, a wider range is usually the more practical choice because it is less likely to go inactive after a modest move. A narrow range can suit someone willing to manage the position and accept that fees may stop when the market leaves the band. The key trade-off is direct: tighter limits concentrate liquidity where it is active, but they make the position more sensitive to price movement.
