Two Cross-Chain Swap Setups: Paying for Speed

A cross-chain swap can save time by paying for priority or liquidity, but gas buys block priority only where it is spent; bridge settlement still sets the pace.

Merkle Street NewsroomOct 24, 2026 · 04:54 UTC#65e2464 min read

A cross-chain swap moves an asset from one blockchain to another by combining a source-chain transaction, a transfer or bridge step, and a destination-chain payout. Two common setups trade cost for speed: pay more for faster transaction inclusion or for liquidity that advances the payout, or use standard fees and wait for the full settlement path.

First, the swap route records what you want to send, what you want to receive, and the destination address. The source transaction then moves the input asset to a bridge contract or a protocol’s deposit address. Validators or other network operators observe that deposit and wait for the source chain to confirm it. The bridge or swap protocol arranges the destination asset, and a transaction sends it to your address. Each stage can add time and fees.

Gas pays for transactions on a particular chain. A higher priority fee can help a transaction get included sooner when that chain is busy. It does not make another chain confirm faster, and it cannot remove a bridge’s own waiting period. A protocol may offer a separate paid fast path; the fuller chainflip explainer covers options this comparison only sketches.

How does a faster cross-chain swap setup work?

A faster setup pays to shorten one or more stages in the route. You may raise the source transaction’s priority fee so it enters a block sooner. If the destination transaction is also waiting for inclusion, the operator may pay higher gas there too. Some protocols offer a paid acceleration option: liquidity providers advance the destination asset while the source deposit completes its confirmation process.

That last option changes who waits. In a standard route, the protocol waits for enough source-chain confirmations before releasing the destination asset. With liquidity advanced, a provider supplies the destination funds earlier, then receives the source-side value after the required checks. The user pays an extra fee for that service. It is a little like paying a courier to bring a parcel from available stock while the original shipment is still on its way; the accounting and confirmation steps still have to finish.

Speed is useful when the market may move before settlement or when the funds are needed soon. But compare the quoted output after all fees, not just the displayed gas estimate. Faster inclusion cannot guarantee a better exchange rate, and a faster payout can cost more than the time saved is worth.

How does the standard-fee setup save money?

A standard-fee setup uses the normal transaction fee and lets each stage proceed at its usual pace. The source transaction waits for ordinary block inclusion, the bridge or protocol waits for its confirmation threshold, and the destination payout uses the network’s regular fee conditions.

This can be the better choice when timing is flexible. It avoids paying extra for priority or an advance from liquidity providers. It does not make the route free: the source transaction still costs gas, the destination payout may have a separate network fee, and the bridge or swap venue may charge its own fee or spread. For some routes, a fast option affects only one stage, so a slow confirmation elsewhere can still determine the total wait.

What should you compare before choosing a setup?

Compare the complete route in the same units: expected amount received, total fees, and estimated settlement time. Check which stage the speed option actually changes. A faster source transaction may help if the source chain is congested; it will not help much if the bridge’s confirmation rule is the bottleneck.

  • Confirm the source and destination networks match the addresses you selected.
  • Compare the final quoted output after gas, protocol fees, and any speed fee.
  • Check whether the speed option advances the payout or only prioritises a transaction.
  • Keep the transaction details and destination address available until settlement completes.

For most readers who do not need the funds immediately, the standard-fee setup is the sensible starting point: it avoids buying speed before you know where the delay lies. Pay for acceleration when the time saved matters to you and the quote shows exactly what that extra cost changes.
