Why Bitcoin Becomes a Token on Another Chain

Wrapped Bitcoin works with another chain’s apps because a bridge holds BTC and issues a matching token, whose redemption depends on the bridge working.

Merkle Street NewsroomOct 16, 2026 · 01:39 UTC#4e56a54 min read

Bitcoin becomes a token on another chain when a bridge holds BTC on Bitcoin and issues a matching token on the destination chain. The original BTC stays on Bitcoin; the new token is a claim designed to represent it elsewhere. That lets Bitcoin value move through systems that Bitcoin itself does not run, such as Ethereum smart contracts.

The process starts when a user chooses a destination address and sends BTC to an address or script controlled by the bridge. A bridge network watches Bitcoin for that deposit and waits for enough confirmations to treat it as settled. It then sends proof of the deposit to a contract on the destination chain. The contract checks the proof, or relies on the bridge’s authorized signers to approve it, then mints tokens to the user’s destination address. Think of it as leaving a valuable item with a locker service and receiving a claim ticket that works in a different building. The ticket can change hands there, but it is not the original item. With Bitcoin, the BTC stays under the bridge’s control while the token moves between compatible wallets and applications.

Why turn Bitcoin into a token?

A token can follow the rules of its destination chain, so apps there can use it. Bitcoin can be held and transferred on its own network, but it cannot directly call an Ethereum lending contract or trade in an Ethereum-based order book. A wrapped token can, because it is issued as a token the destination chain understands.

This makes BTC usable as collateral, a trading asset, or a source of liquidity in applications built on another chain. The token does not make Bitcoin itself faster or add smart contracts to Bitcoin. It creates a separate representation that those applications can handle. The trade-off is that users rely on the bridge and the destination token contract as well as on Bitcoin.

How does a wrapped Bitcoin token get redeemed?

To return to Bitcoin, the holder sends the destination token to a contract that burns it or marks it for redemption, and names a Bitcoin address. The bridge network observes that action, checks that it is valid and not already used, then authorizes BTC to be sent from its Bitcoin-controlled funds to that address. In a burn-and-release design, destroying the token reduces the outstanding supply as BTC leaves the bridge.

That link between tokens and reserves is the mechanism behind the claim. The bridge must keep enough BTC available and prevent the same deposit from authorizing multiple mints. Designs differ in who controls the Bitcoin keys and how the destination chain learns that a deposit or burn happened. RenBridge is one example of a system built around this cross-chain process; its specific rules are covered in the linked explainer.

What should you check before using wrapped BTC?

Check which token contract and bridge you are using. A token called “BTC” on one chain may not be interchangeable with a similarly named token on another. Before depositing or redeeming, confirm that the destination network and wallet address are supported, and understand how the bridge authorizes withdrawals.

The main points to check are:

  • Backing: Find out what holds the BTC and how the token supply is matched to it.
  • Control: Check who can authorize transfers from the Bitcoin reserves and what happens if bridge operators or validators fail.
  • Redemption: Confirm the return process, fees, destination address requirements, and any waiting period.

For most readers, wrapped BTC makes sense when a specific application on another chain is worth the added bridge dependency. If you only need to hold or send Bitcoin, native BTC avoids that extra claim and its supporting system.
